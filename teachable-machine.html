Project Statement

Overview & Project Goals

For Project 3, Eddie and I built a five-class image classification model using Google Teachable Machine to explore how machine learning systems categorize everyday object, and where they break down. Rather than choosing a abstract topic, we trained our classifier on five easy items from daily life: hats, shirts, sweatshirts, shoes, and random objects. Because clothes and everyday items are so familiar, they gave us an easy way to test how an algorithm reads shapes, colors, and textures through a webcam. Throughout the project, we collaborated together collecting and organizing our 200-image dataset, training and testing the model in Teachable Machine, and building out our Project 3 webpage to document our process and connect it to the rest of our site.

Training a model to tell a sweatshirt apart from a show might seem simple compared to facial recognition or hiring software, but building it showed us that even a small class project runs on the exact same logic as big commercial computer vision tools. As Dr.Joy Buolamwini explains in _Unmasking AI_, machine learning models don't actually "know" what an object is the way a person does, an artificial neural network is just a "pattern recognizer" that adjusts its weights based on the labeled examples it gets shown. Watching where our model worked and where it failed in a low-stakes setting helped us see how human choices during data collection directly shape what an algorithm can and can't see.

Data Collection & Curation

To build an original, balanced dataset, Eddie and I each took 100 photos, 20 per category, and combined them for 200 training images total. Splitting the photos evenly between us was a deliberate choice. If we had only used clothes from one place under one lighting, the model probably would have memorized a single wall color, specific logos, or one room's lighting instead of what actually makes a hat a hat. Mixing our photos across two different places, closets, and camera angles gave the model much more variety in lighting, fabrics, styles, and backgrounds.

Even with 200 original images, deciding what went into each folder forced us to make judgement calls that affected how the model behaved. In Chapter 10 of _Unmasking AI_, Buolamwini points out that whoever builds a dataset acts as the "arbiters of ground truth," noting that "decision-making power is ultimately what defines ground truth" and "human decisions are subjective."
